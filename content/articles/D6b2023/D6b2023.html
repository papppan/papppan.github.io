Most beauty ful baryon-baryon scattering

Dr. Padmanath Madanagopalan
15 March 2023

From the Bigbang nucleosynthesis to the formation of big atomic nuclei, from the terrestrial hadron-hadron collider experiments to the complex dynamics within the cores of stellar environments, baryon-baryon interactions play a fundamental role. They are the remnant interactions or the Van der Waals forces between colorless protons and neutrons (otherwise known as nucleons) arising from the hidden fundamental strong interaction dynamics within the nucleons. Note that all of the energy that get released in fusion or fission reactions are the binding energies arising out of such baryon-baryon interactions. Scientists worldwide have been trying to interpret the abundant particle scattering data from collider experiments as well as to understand the observed astrophysical and cosmological data for several decades. These studies all essentially point to a deeper understanding of interactions between baryons. Such an understanding also can have indirect implications in neutrino physics and dark matter physics, in building effective field theories to describe nuclear many-body dynamics within large nuclei reactions involved in the experimental searches. Thus understanding baryon-baryon interactions play a pivotal role in a multidisciplinary perspective.

Dibaryons are subatomic particles that allow the study of baryon-baryon interactions in their quintessential forms. Nucleons are composed of three quarks (namely up and down quarks, which are referred to as light quarks). On the other hand, dibaryons are formed out of six quarks and have baryon-number twice that of a proton. Quantum ChromoDynamics (QCD), the fundamental theory of strong interactions, allows dibaryons to take any form of six-quark states, whether it be a compact six-quark state, a molecular two-baryon state or something else. Just like the ephemeral heavy cousins of nucleons (Λ, Λc, Λb, etc.), that are built out of other heavier quark flavors (strange, charm and beauty), dibaryons could also be formed out of different quark flavors. While we have rich experimental data on nucleon-nucleon scattering from terrestrial experiments that are studied towards the search for light dibaryons, there is no scattering data available on baryon-baryon scattering involving strange or other heavy quark flavors. In conditions of large nuclear densities, such as those that could exist in the cores of neutron stars, supernovae, white dwarfs, etc., hyperon (baryons with strange quarks) formation is favored. This implies there could be observable implications on the thermodynamics of inner cores of stellar objects, from hyperon-nucleon and hyperon-hyperon interactions. Understanding these interactions is important for developing a complete understanding of the behavior of matter in extreme conditions, such as those found in the cores of neutron stars and supernovae.

Despite decades of experimental efforts, the only stable dibaryon known to exist is the Deuteron. There exists experimental evidence for the existence of only one other dibaryon resonance [d*(2380)]. Dibaryon with nonzero strange flavor (the H-dibaryon) has long been conjectured by Robert L. Jaffe from MIT, although there is no clear and conclusive evidence for its existence [1]. Several theoretical investigations using phenomenologically motivated Quantum Mechanical potentials and effective field theories were built to understand, to interpret the existing experimental data and make predictions (c.f. Refs. [2]). Nonetheless, it is essential to investigate baryon-baryon interactions at the fundamental level of strong interactions to have a deeper understanding. A handful of such investigations on baryon-baryon interactions and dibaryons have also been made in the light and strange quark flavor sectors, such as those using lattice QCD [3]. Lattice QCD is a computationally intensive methodology to investigate the properties of the theory by defining it on a space-time grid (or lattice, such as in the video above), followed by numerical Monte Carlo simulations. Most of the theoretical investigations are concentrated in the light and the strange quark sector, as the experimental prospects for heavy baryons are dampened by the short lifetimes of baryons with heavy quarks. Even so, the results on baryon-baryon interactions at very heavy quark masses (such as with charm and beauty quark flavors) are intriguing and can shed interesting insights into the physics of baryonic forces.

In a recent publication in Physical Review Letters, Padmanath Madanagopalan from The Institute of Mathematical Sciences Chennai, along with his collaborators Nilmani Mathur and Debsubhra Chakraborty from TIFR Mumbai report a first-principles calculation of two Ωbbb baryons (baryons entirely made of beauty quarks) scattering from the fundamental theory (See the above video for an artistic view). They find unambiguous evidence for strong attractive interaction between the Ωbbb baryons, pointing to the possibility of a deeply bound dibaryon state referred to as 𝒟6b (See figure above for a schematic picture). Working with state-of-the-art statistical ensembles of the theory defined on the lattice, the best-suited formulation for the heavy beauty quark dynamics, and advanced computational and complex analysis procedures, the authors determine the binding energy of such a state to be ~81 MeV (~40 times that of the Deuteron) with unprecedented control over the systematic uncertainties. This finding elucidates the intriguing feature of strong forces in baryon-baryon interactions at the beauty quark mass. It also gives great motivation for further systematic studies of quark mass dependence in two-baryon interactions to build a comprehensive picture ranging from the very heavy quark masses down to the light quark masses, which perhaps can shed more insights into the emergence of bindings in nuclei. Although the lifetimes of heavy baryons are expected to be extremely small, this finding also calls for searches in future generation experiments for such heavier exotic subatomic particles.

First-principles investigations of particle interactions such as this require a detailed knowledge of quantum field theory and large-scale High-Performance Computing (HPC) facility at disposal. Lattice gauge theorists such as Padmanath and Nilmani utilize some of the largest HPC facilities across the globe, such as those in Germany (GCS/NIC Julich), the US (NESRC), India (ILGTI) to investigate the different facets of nuclear interactions that operate at the scale of femto-Universe (0.000000000000001 meters). The large interaction strength of the strong force at this scale prohibits any acceptable analytic solutions using first principles from fundamental theory limiting one to resort to numerical procedures with huge HPC requirements. Working with such facilities, the authors look forward to performing several other cutting-edge calculations that can shed light on hadron-hadron interactions, eventually leading to a deeper quark-level understanding of the hadron exotics.
